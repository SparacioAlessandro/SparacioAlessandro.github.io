I am a Research Scientist and Principal Investigator at A*STAR's Institute for Human Development and Potential in Singapore. My work asks a deceptively simple question: which low-cost mental-health interventions actually work, for whom, and through what mechanism?

I led a 37-site study of self-administered mindfulness with 2,239 participants, published in Nature Human Behaviour and selected as the issue cover article.

That study also exposed a problem the field had been living with: without standardised materials and properly matched control conditions, mindfulness results are difficult to compare and harder to replicate. So I built SOMA — an open stimulus library designed to fix exactly that, now freely available for any research group to use.

My doctoral research at Université Grenoble Alpes and Swansea University produced two meta-analyses on stress regulation, the second as a Registered Report. I earned my Ph.D. with distinction, cited in part for open science practice. Since joining A*STAR I have moved into clinical trials and digital biomarkers, including a fully decentralised pilot using heart-rate variability to measure stress remotely.

Alongside the research, I work on reproducibility infrastructure for the region — because tools and standards outlast any individual study.

Research Programme

One question, pursued at three levels: run trials large and rigorous enough to trust, build the shared materials that make those trials comparable, and make sure both reach populations that mindfulness research has historically left out.

REFORM

Building Resilience Across Socioeconomic Contexts: A Multi-Site Study on Self-Administered Mindfulness Interventions

Most mindfulness trials test whether a practice works. REFORM asks a harder question: does the structure of the practice matter — formal seated sessions versus informal practice woven into daily life — and does that answer change across socioeconomic contexts? The trial runs across multiple international sites using the SOMA library, so every site delivers materially identical materials.

SOMA

A free, open stimulus library for mindfulness research — with the matched active controls the field has been missing.

Free to use CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 Validated

Standardized Open-source Mindfulness Architecture

If you run mindfulness studies, you know the two hardest problems: everyone builds their own materials, and almost nobody has a control condition that holds attention constant without smuggling mindfulness back in. SOMA solves both. Every mindfulness script is paired with a structurally matched active control — same length, same narrator, same pacing, different cognitive task — and the whole library is free, documented and ready to drop into your protocol.

It was also built for participants usually excluded from this research: Grade 5–6 reading level, no quiet room required, no prior experience assumed, secular throughout.

Selected Publications

Three papers that trace the arc: establish the effect, build better tools, test it in the real world.

Nature Human Behaviour2024Cover article

Self-administered mindfulness interventions reduce stress in a large, randomized controlled multi-site study

Mindfulness apps are everywhere, but the evidence base was thin and fragmented. We tested four brief exercises across 37 sites and 2,239 participants — the largest study of its kind — to establish what a short, self-guided practice actually does to stress.

View publication
JMIR Mental Health2025Pilot RCT

Feasibility and acceptability of a smartphone-delivered mindfulness intervention for stress reduction in adult Singaporeans

A fully decentralised pilot: participants used their own phones, at home, with no researcher contact. It tested whether remote delivery and remote stress measurement could work at all — the groundwork for running trials at scale.

View publication
Collabra: Psychology2023Meta-analysis

Stress regulation via being in nature and social support in adults: A meta-analysis

Two of the most commonly recommended ways to manage stress, assessed against the full published literature — including how much of the apparent effect survives correction for publication bias.

A Small Tool

Not part of my research, and not a clinical intervention — just a paced-breathing timer, built because people kept asking for one. Slow, controlled breathing is associated with reductions in self-reported stress and arousal in short trials; the effects are modest and the mechanisms still debated.
